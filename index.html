Elder care · 2030 – 2035

By the mid-2030s, there will be more people over 80 than babies.
Who will care for them?

Not humanoids, at least not at first. The care gap will be filled in layers: sensors and AI in the home first, then task robots, and only then physical AI, meaning machines with a brain of their own. Here is what is real, what is likely, and what is still hype.

01 · The care gap

The demand is already born. The carers are not.

Everyone who will be 80+ in 2035 is alive today, so these are among the safest numbers in any forecast. The open question is who looks after them.

Labor, not money, is the limit

Japan and Taiwan can pay for care but cannot staff it. In Japan, care jobs had about 3.9 openings per applicant in August 2026, against about 1.1 for all jobs. Care workers earn roughly 79% of the average wage. Sourced, secondary

Families carry the hidden load

In the EU, unpaid family care is worth about 2.5% of GDP. That is more than public long-term-care spending, which is forecast to rise from 1.7% to 2.6% of GDP by 2070. Sourced (EC via OECD)

Taiwan's clock is fast

Taiwan has about 3.2 workers per older person in 2026, and the ratio is falling. Its dementia population is about 350k and is expected to pass 470k by 2031. Forecast (NDC / MOHW)

Note: Japan's 570k figure is a need calculated on today's care model. It is not a forecast of unfilled jobs, and no official number exists for how many of those jobs technology could remove.

02 · Know the difference

A robot is not the same as physical AI

People say "robot" for both, but they are different machines. The difference decides what can safely care for a person.

Physical AI is a device with an autonomous brain. It perceives, reasons, plans and acts on its own, and it recovers when the world changes. A conventional robot follows a fixed program or a human operator.

Conventional robot

Body, no brain of its own. It repeats the same steps very reliably. It gets confused when the room, the person or the task changes.

  • PARO therapy seal
  • Lifting and transfer robots (RIBA/Robear, Fuji "Hug")
  • Panasonic Resyone bed-to-wheelchair
  • Automated pill dispensers
  • Any humanoid while a remote human drives it

Ambient AI

Brain, no body. It senses and reasons but cannot move anything. This third category will do most of the work in 2030.

  • mmWave radar fall detection (no camera)
  • ElliQ-type voice companions
  • Remote patient monitoring, wearables
  • LLM health and memory agents

Physical AI

Brain plus body. An on-board foundation model (vision-language-action or world model) sees the scene, understands the goal, plans and acts, and recovers from surprises.

  • Foxconn/Kawasaki Nurabot (hospital wards, Taiwan)
  • Fourier GR-3 Care-bot
  • Figure 03 with Helix
  • 1X NEO, when it runs autonomously
  • Zuowei "Tianshu"/"Tianji" nursing humanoids (China)

The teleoperation catch

A humanoid that a remote person is steering is a conventional robot at that moment, even if it looks futuristic. The first home humanoids, such as 1X NEO, rely on remote human operators for difficult tasks. So the useful question is not "is it autonomous?" but "what share of task time is autonomous?" Our view

03 · How it arrives

Three layers, not one robot

The limit is not how smart the AI is. It is safety validation, reimbursement, unit cost and fitting into how carers actually work.

Now

Sensors & software

Monitoring, fall detection, voice agents, facility logistics robots. In Japan, about 47% of surveyed facilities already use monitoring devices. Sourced (MHLW 2026)

2027–2030

Task robots

Mobility assist, transfer support, rehabilitation exoskeletons, hospital delivery robots, night patrols. Forecast

2030+

Contact-care humanoids

Humanoids in selected roles, where the economics and safety certification both work out. Institutions in Japan, China and Taiwan go first. Forecast

04 · What robots and physical AI will actually do

Task by task: 2030 vs 2035

Look at the failures first. In one Japanese care home, staff rejected a lifting robot because it moved residents "like a piece of luggage" (James Wright, Robots Won't Save Japan, 2023). Many purchased robots were used for a few weeks and then locked away. Adoption is high for sensors and low for robots that touch people. That split is the key fact.

Who is building it (status, November 2026)

Foxconn Nurabot · Taiwan

Built with Kawasaki and Taichung Veterans General Hospital on NVIDIA Jetson Orin. Foxconn reports 75–80 tasks a day and about 30% less nursing workload, with 2–3 hours a day saved per nurse. It is expanding to more hospitals and long-term-care sites under "Healthy Taiwan." Vendor claim

1X NEO · home humanoid

$20,000, or $499/month. Preorders opened October 2025. A factory with 10,000-unit-a-year capacity opened April 2026. As of mid-October 2026 we found no confirmed customer deliveries, and the robot relies on remote human operators for hard tasks. Sourced

Fourier GR-3 Care-bot

A 165 cm care humanoid with about 3 hours of runtime, aimed first at eldercare facilities and hospitals. It is announced but not yet in wide commercial use. Prices quoted by resellers range from about $100k to $250k. Unverified pricing

ElliQ · New York State

The NY State Office for the Aging has run an ElliQ program since 2022 and reports a large drop in loneliness. The data are self-reported, co-published with the vendor, and have no control group. Vendor-linked data

Tesla Optimus & Figure 03

Optimus is not commercialized, and Tesla's timelines have slipped repeatedly. Figure says it had delivered 350+ units by April 2026, mainly to factories such as BMW Spartanburg; home use is still in alpha. Sourced

China's national pilot

A 2025–2027 elder-care robot pilot requires each home product to be tested in at least 200 households. Shenzhen Zuowei showed humanoid nursing robots in June 2026. Sourced

05 · Medical breakthroughs

The quiet revolution: catching decline earlier

For care demand, the biggest change by 2030 is probably not a cure. It is cheap, early diagnosis plus prevention, which moves care into the home years earlier.

06 · Inside the machine

Memory and touch, not intelligence, are the bottleneck

The brain

NVIDIA Jetson Thor delivers about 2,070 FP4 TFLOPS but only 273 GB/s of memory bandwidth. A 7B-parameter robot model can take longer to read its own weights than one 20 ms control loop allows. Bandwidth, not TOPS, limits the robot. Omdia analysis

The sense of touch

A humanoid carries 6–12 cameras, radar, force/torque sensors in its wrists and ankles, about 28 joint-torque sensors and tactile skin. Low-cost force and touch sensing is not solved at scale, and elder care needs exactly that: gentle, precise force. Omdia analysis

The cost curve

Total sensor cost per humanoid is forecast to fall from about $3,500 today to about $350 by 2035. Solid-state lidar falls from about $5,000 to about $50. Consumer humanoids fall below $20k around 2028. Forecast (Omdia)

The battery

About 3 hours of runtime (GR-3) is a care-specific weakness. A night patrol needs 8+ hours or automatic docking. Our view

07 · Money & policy

Care is the wedge market, because Asia subsidizes it

The eldercare robot market is estimated to grow from about $3.6B (2026) to $10B+ (2035). Forecast (Omdia)

Japan

METI has a ¥387.3B FY2026 physical-AI budget and a target of about 10 million AI robots by 2040. A 44-member national platform is targeting operations in 2028. The health ministry has historically been more skeptical than the industry ministry, which has slowed adoption.

China

The State Council's 2025 elder-care reform guidelines, the 2025–2027 robot pilot and the January 2026 measures support putting rehab and nursing robots inside care institutions. The silver economy is officially a "key pillar" after 2035.

Taiwan

Long-Term Care 3.0 targets "10-minute care circles," under one day from hospital discharge to long-term care, and wider dementia eligibility. Foxconn's robots run inside the "Healthy Taiwan" program.

US & EU

US Medicare pays for remote monitoring but not for humanoid or companion robots. The EU AI Act's high-risk rules start in Dec 2027 (Annex III) and Aug 2028 (AI in regulated products), after the Digital Omnibus delay.

Our view: payment follows measured outcomes. Expect monitoring to be reimbursed by 2030 and task robots to be paid for by facility labor savings. Home humanoids will need subscriptions (like NEO's $499/month) or long-term-care insurance products that do not yet exist.

08 · Ethics & the human touch

Dignity first, then efficiency

Dignity

Robots that touch people must be designed with carers, not for them. What carers value most is time for relationship, not task speed.

Surveillance

Ambient sensing is the most useful near-term technology and also the most invasive. Process video and health data on the device, inside the home. With teleoperated robots a stranger can see into the home, so consent and logging are essential.

Honest companions

AI companions create attachment. They should always say they are machines, and they should never fake a human relationship to increase engagement. EU AI Act transparency rules apply from August 2026.

Jobs

The evidence points away from mass job loss before 2035. One NBER study found robot-adopting Japanese nursing homes had 3–8% more staff. The real risks are deskilling and closer monitoring of workers.

09 · A day in the life

Mrs. Lin, 85, Taipei

One person, two futures. Both are scenarios built from the evidence above. Our view

2030

A ceiling radar logs her sleep and walking overnight, with no camera. Her voice companion reminds her about her heart-health medicine and records her blood pressure from a cuffless wearable. A blood test two years ago flagged early amyloid, so a monthly memory check runs through the agent. A smart dispenser releases her pills.

A human aide visits three times a week, and a robot vacuum does the floors. If she falls, the radar calls for help within seconds, but a person still has to come and lift her. In hospital, Nurabot-type robots bring her medicine while nurses do the touching. There is no humanoid in her home.

2035

Her daughter leases a home humanoid for perhaps $300–500 a month (our estimate, not a published forecast). It tidies, does laundry, fetches things and steadies her on the stairs. Getting her out of bed still uses a powered lift or a carer.

At her day-care center, certified physical AI robots help with transfers and night patrols. Her companion has long-term memory and talks to her doctors' AI. Her biggest gain since 2030 is not the robot. Her dementia was caught five years earlier, and her frailty was delayed.

10 · Contrarian takes

What the hype gets wrong

Ambient AI plus simple robots beats humanoids until at least 2032

About 47% of surveyed Japanese facilities use monitoring devices, while contact robots stalled near 10%. The cobot and mobile-robot wave is already here. The humanoid story is early.

Drugs and diagnostics may cut demand more than robots add supply

Every year that frailty is delayed removes a year of 24/7 care. The counterpoint: GLP-1s failed in symptomatic Alzheimer's, and muscle loss is a real risk for the very old.

The bottleneck is memory and touch, not intelligence

Robot DRAM is getting expensive and force/tactile sensing is not industrialized. Elder care needs gentle, precise force more than any factory job does.

Teleoperation is the hidden business model

Early home humanoids are partly remote-controlled. They are labor arbitrage (remote carers) before they become labor replacement.

Demand independent proof

The 30% nursing-workload cut and the large loneliness drops come from vendors or vendor partners, without control groups. They are encouraging, but they are not yet proven.
